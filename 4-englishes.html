Eight thousand years in one sentence

One Sentence, Four Englishes

The phrase, in today's English “They poured a huge amount of hard work into the document, but he just focussed on the name of the language.”

The Englishes — the written record
Modern English
c. 1700 – today
the sentence you started with

They poured a huge amount of hard work into the document, but he just focussed on the name of the language.

What changed Spelling is standardised, the do but construction is gone, and the Latin loan focus — borrowed in the 1600s for optics and only later meaning “attention” — has ousted bend one's mind. Fittingly, the very word he fixated on is one of the newest arrivals in the sentence.

Early Modern English
c. 1500 – 1700
Shakespeare · the King James Bible

They powred a mighty deale of hard labour into the paper, but he did but bend his minde upon the name of the tongue.

What changed The Great Vowel Shift has quietly re‑tuned the long vowels, and Caxton's printing press is beginning to freeze spelling — though deale, minde and powred still wobble from one page to the next. The tell‑tale did but bend construction and words like tongue for “language” place this squarely in the age of the playhouse and the pulpit. You can now read it without a dictionary.

Middle English
c. 1150 – 1500
Chaucer · after the Normans

Thei pourede greet travaille and harde swynk into the book, but he oonly sette his herte upon the name of the langage.

gist “They poured great labour and hard toil into the book, but he only set his heart upon the name of the language.”

What changed The Norman Conquest let French flood in: pourede, travaille and langage now sit right beside native swynk and herte. The old case endings have collapsed into a general unstressed ‑e, and spelling is nobody's fixed business yet. Read it aloud and you'll half‑understand it — which is the whole difference between a foreign language and an ancestral one. Going back in time, this is as far as our singular they reaches: the pronoun is itself a Norse import (þeir) that ousted native hīe, used for a single, unspecified person only since the 1300s — which is why the Old English row just below must fall back on “she.”

Old English
c. 450 – 1150
Beowulf · the Anglo-Saxons

Gēat micel geswinc and heard weorc on þæt gewrit, ac hē āna hōgode ymbe þone naman þære spræce.

word‑for‑word “[They] poured much toil and hard work onto the writing, but he alone heeded the name of the speech.” (Old English had no gender‑neutral singular pronoun, so the worker is left unspoken — the verb gēat carries them, no gender imposed. A subjectless clause like this was only marginally natural in Old English: the honest price of not forcing a gender.)

What you're seeing A fully inflected Germanic tongue — closer to Old Norse or German than to us. The letter þ (thorn) spells th; case endings do the grammatical heavy lifting (þone naman, accusative; þære spræce, genitive “of the language”). Every content word is native Germanic: gewrit (a thing written), spræc (speech), geswinc (toil). Not one French or Latin borrowing has arrived yet.

Deep roots — before there was English
Nobody wrote these down: every form is reconstructed by comparing the descendant languages. Scholars normally flag that with a leading asterisk, dropped here at your request — reconstructed words appear in violet italics instead. The sentences are considered compositions in the tradition of Schleicher's Fable: the roots are real, but endings and word order are debatable. And since none of these tongues had a gender‑neutral singular pronoun at all (that only arrives with Middle English “they”), the hard worker is never given a pronoun: in every line the verb ending alone carries them — a “null subject” — while the single overt pronoun is spent only on the contrastive “he.” That imposes no gender, and it happens to be exactly how a pro‑drop language marks the topic it isn't contrasting.
Proto‑West‑Germanic
c. 1 – 400 CE
the eve of English

Gaut felu hardes werkes in þat giwrit, ak hē āna hogdē umbi þana namon þerā sprākā.

sense “[They] poured much hard work into the writing, but he heeded only the name of the speech.” Almost a dialect of the line above — only a few centuries separate them — yet already tilting toward the Old English on the next row.

WhenThe first few centuries CE, just before the migrations to Britain.
WhereThe North Sea coastlands — the Low Countries and north‑west Germany — homeland of the Angles, Saxons, Jutes and Frisians before the boats set out.
WhoThe West Germanic tribes on the point of scattering: the shared parent of English, Frisian, Dutch and German.
Its reachThe nearest common ancestor English shares with its close cousins across the Channel.

Did you know English's closest living relative isn't German but Frisian, still spoken on the Dutch and German coast. The old rhyme “Butter, bread and green cheese is good English and good Fries” — Fries the old word for a Frisian, which is what lets it rhyme with cheese — runs almost unchanged in the Frisian original, “Bûter, brea en griene tsiis is goed Ingelsk en goed Frysk.” A family likeness that has outlasted fifteen centuries apart.

Proto‑Germanic
c. 500 BCE – 1 CE
the Germanic branch begins

Gaut felu hardas werkas in þat gawritą, ak iz aina hugdē umbi þana namōn þaizōz sprēkōz.

sense “[They] poured much hard work into the writing, but he heeded only the name of the speech.” Runes are just appearing by now, so gawritą ‘a written thing’ is fair game — it runs straight down to Old English gewrit.

WhenThe Iron Age — the last few centuries BCE.
WhereSouthern Scandinavia and the North German plain — around modern Denmark, southern Sweden and northern Germany.
WhoThe early Germanic peoples, before they split into the ancestors of English, German, Dutch, the Nordic languages and long‑dead Gothic.
Its reachThe common trunk of every Germanic language living or extinct.

Did you know This branch is defined by Grimm's Law — yes, that Grimm, of the fairy tales — a chain of sound shifts that turned Indo‑European p, t, k into f, th, h. It's why Latin pēs and English foot, or Latin trēs and English three, are the very same word wearing different consonants.

Proto‑Indo‑European
c. 4500 – 2500 BCE
the deepest root

Pélu kr̥túm wérǵom en gr̥bʰóm ǵʰéwet, at só óynom nómn̥ dn̥ǵʰwés ménet.

sense “Poured much hard work into the scratched‑thing, but he minded only the name of the tongue.” A composition in the spirit of Schleicher's Fable; with no writing yet in existence, “document” is carried by the root that later gave both Greek gráphō ‘write’ and English carve. The tireless worker is simply the dropped subject — no pronoun needed.

WhenLate Stone Age into the early Bronze Age — roughly six to eight thousand years ago.
WhereThe Pontic–Caspian steppe north of the Black and Caspian Seas (today's Ukraine and southern Russia). A 2025 ancient‑DNA study nudges the very earliest root a little south, to a “Caucasus–Lower Volga” people.
WhoSteppe pastoralists — the Yamnaya herders and their forebears — whose language travelled outward with them on horse and cart.
Its reachAncestor of languages now spoken by nearly half the planet: English, Spanish, Russian, Hindi, Persian and Greek among them.

Did you know Not a single word survives in writing — the entire language is inferred. The method is so precise that in 1879 Ferdinand de Saussure argued certain lost sounds must once have existed; decades later the decipherment of Hittite proved him right, confirming sounds that had been predicted before anyone alive had heard them.

A family, not a relay race
The column above followed only English's own thread. But its ancestors had other children: here is where the cousins branch away from the same Proto-Germanic and West Germanic nodes. Time runs left (ancient) to right (modern); English's own line is picked out in red.

A deliberately simplified tree — the internal grouping of West Germanic (especially where Low German sits) is genuinely debated, and each leaf hides dozens of dialects. The one firm headline: English's nearest living relative is Frisian, not German.

A note on honesty: the four English versions are period‑faithful pastiches rather than sentences any scribe truly wrote, though the grammar, spelling and vocabulary of each are the real article; the three deep‑root lines are scholarly compositions assembled from reconstructed roots, with debatable endings. Dates and homelands for the older stages are estimates that still shift with new evidence.
